# A v6 research plan for opponent inference in four-player Blokus

November 17, 2026. Proposed extension to the native search line. The historical v6 native-engine and learned-prior experiments retain their existing names. The experiment labels below describe work to be tested.

The first controlled opponent-policy replay is runnable on a CPU. [PC runbook](../docs/V6_OPPONENT_REPLAY.md)

## The claim worth testing

Given the same board and the same clock, can Blokee choose a better move because it saw *how these three opponents played*? The target is a probability distribution over observable legal replies. Several private heuristics can explain the same play. Success means predicting consequential differences and gaining points when exploiting them. A style label can help explain a result, but a correct label earns no points by itself.

Consider a move that opens a second route into the center while exposing Blokee's only useful corner for the straight triomino. Another move preserves that corner but concedes territory. If the next seat has repeatedly taken comparable denial opportunities, the first line may lose its only fit before our turn returns. If the same seat usually develops its own frontier when offered those choices, the first line may be better. Replay both moves, including the other two seats' replies, and compare their final score distributions. This example is hypothetical. The recent Pro logs make piece survival a concrete first intervention to study. [Native tier benchmark](../docs/BENCHMARK.md)

## What the project has already learned

- The K experiment trained a frozen population policy plus a player-specific residual. On held-out variants of **stable synthetic personalities**, after three prior observations the selected 32-unit GRU improved next-action log loss by 0.150 [0.122, 0.178] nats/action over the population and 0.248 [0.207, 0.289] over shuffled histories. Top-five recall was 83.0%, calibration error 0.048. Four moves after a spliced style change it recovered the new family only 32.3% of the time. Family Bayes remains an important inexpensive comparator. [K results](v5_oracle_player_residual.md)
- The change detector in L reached 74.3% four-move recovery on variant c, but stable-player loss worsened by 0.175 nats/action and calibration error reached 0.120. Training switches all occurred at turn six. The later randomized development probe removed the turn-six spike, but its gate still opened on stable games. On a separate CPU league, adding board context improved the generic predictor by 0.214 nats while correct history beat shuffled history by only 0.0018, with an interval spanning zero. Source diversity and the model's ability to ignore uninformative histories matter. [L results and randomized probe](v5_oracle_change_point_preregistered.md) · [CPU context experiment](v5_oracle_context_policy.md)
- In practical play the first one-lap reranker cost 4.20× at the small test budget and scored −9.15 [−12.00, −6.26] against v4.1. The later full-distribution correction improved that comparison to −1.06 [−2.93, +0.76], still without an established strength gain. The old synthetic league, export format, controls and real-history tracker are valuable starting points. [Response experiments D–F](v5_oracle_foundation.md) · [Runtime tracker](../src/ai/oracle/runtime.ts)

## Model and runtime

**Observer.** Share a board and legal-placement encoder across seats. Inputs include remaining hands, turn order, relative position, and which opponent would lose an attachment point under each placement. Encode the chosen move relative to its legal alternatives. A forced pass carries board information and gives no preference evidence. Keep three separate per-game memories. Update only the acting seat's style evidence after its observed action, and update the shared interaction context after every real move. On undo or a corrected import, rebuild from the accepted move prefix. No persistent player identifier is needed.

**Predictor.** Reuse K's population scorer and K GRU and family Bayes as baselines. For the new candidate compare a stable-history expert with a recent-history expert. Maintain a distribution over whether recent moves are better explained by the existing behavior or a change. Update it from the likelihood each expert assigned to the move *before* it was played. Include a stable-for-the-whole-game hypothesis. Match changed and stable samples at the same turn and board phase. Fit temperatures, mixture strength, and switch priors only on development games. Compare with a turn-only gate to catch another clock shortcut. A Bayesian run-length filter gives an explicit competing design; fit it to the model's board-conditioned action probabilities and validate its assumptions empirically. [Adams & MacKay, Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.3742)

One implementable form is `logit_j(s,a) = base(s,a) + reliability_j × dot(features(s,a), weights_j)`, normalized over all legal placements. The three vectors of weights are small outputs of the history model. Their values change during the game while the network parameters stay frozen. Use explicit features for quantities that should have names, such as denying the next seat a corner, plus learned features if they improve prediction. Estimate reliability on development data from predictive evidence, disagreement and unfamiliar positions. Empty history gives exactly the population prediction. A small legal-action floor covers unexpected moves. Train with next-legal-action log loss, shifting history by one move. Authored family labels may assist synthetic training and diagnosis. Withhold engine names, player IDs, future moves, and switch labels at inference. This adapts the character, current-state and prediction split in ToMnet to a single short game. [Rabinowitz et al., Machine Theory of Mind](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/rabinowitz18a.html)

**Search.** Take one immutable snapshot of the three beliefs for each real decision. In an experimental MCTS arm, draw a plausible policy for each seat at the start of each simulation and keep that draw consistent along its line. At modeled opponent turns sample from that policy over legal replies. At Blokee turns let the incumbent search choose actions using the expected return across hypotheses. Blokee's selector sees only board and public history, so a simulated Blokee cannot choose a different defense just because it secretly knows the sampled profile. Retain a baseline-search component for ordinary strong replies. Begin with the next opponent turn only, then ablate extension through the next three seats. Keep the four-seat terminal scoring. This estimates what happens *if we play a particular move*. It borrows belief sampling from POMCP, with the board fully visible and opponent behavior uncertain. [Silver & Veness, Monte-Carlo Planning in Large POMDPs](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2010/hash/edfbe1afcf9246bb0d40eb4d8027d90f-Abstract.html)

On the research branch, the native hooks are `engine-rs/src/search.rs` for selection and `engine-rs/src/feature_prior.rs` for candidate scoring. Compute histories once per actual move. Cache board features at expansion and avoid a network call per rollout ply. Candidate windows and chance sampling must account for probability outside the window; keep a sampled tail where a full distribution is expensive. When reply probabilities depend on past targeting, key cached distributions and transpositions by the relevant public interaction context or disable those transpositions in the experimental region. Hypothetical placements may change branch-local board and interaction features. They never update real player memory. Verify a zero-strength switch gives identical seeded root decisions to the incumbent.

**Blokee's own weights.** The opponent predictor alone can change our chosen move since it changes the expected return of candidate lines. A separate optional learned controller can also change *our own weights*. As a second ablation, learn a small gated mixture over bounded adjustments to active native root-prior features such as frontier access, corner denial and preserving viable piece placements. Its inputs are the board and the three beliefs with uncertainty. Populate training targets by replaying candidate adjustments from the same training positions, varying opponents and continuing to final four-seat scores on paired seeds. Train to prefer adjustments with higher estimated own advantage, or distill the stronger search's action distribution. Shrink adjustments toward zero with weak evidence. Compare the same controller using board alone and board plus histories. This avoids crediting history for ordinary phase-dependent play. Additions to inactive evaluation fields would have no effect on this path. A learned mixture of experts is an established precedent for conditioning a policy on opponents. [He et al., Opponent Modeling in Deep Reinforcement Learning](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/he16.html)

## Experiment sequence

1. **Build two cheap offline tests first.** Reuse the implemented randomized exporter and the 58,000,000 development block. Mix switched and never-switched seats within the same games. Add new strong and weak unseen engine variants, novel mixtures, ordinary phase changes and independent openings. Keep splits disjoint by game, configuration/player and opening family. First reproduce K, L and the generic baselines. Separately test the *value of knowing the opponent*: on matched midgames with controlled opponents, compare the candidate moves selected with their true policies available versus a population model. Use hidden simulator identities only in this privileged diagnostic. Play each candidate to completion with the actual opponent binaries and fresh continuation seeds. Estimate the deployable margin at stake per game as well as gain on the subset of choices that change. If even the informed chooser finds negligible gain, spend the Pro budget on general search. This is an empirical estimate for the tested planner and opponents.
2. **Qualify the predictor in shadow mode.** Predict before every observed move without influencing it. Compare the generic board policy, same-phase shuffled histories, K, family Bayes, turn-only detection and the new adaptive model. Report gain after 0/3/5/8 observed moves, calibration and harmful high-confidence misses by phase and seat. Carry forward K's historical lower bounds of +0.03 nats/action over fixed and +0.06 over shuffled, top-five recall above 65%, and calibration error at most 0.08. Require stable loss degradation at most +0.01 versus K and recovery above 50% after four observations on labeled switches. Predeclare a stable-game false alarm threshold using development costs. Measure prediction gains and calibration on held-out external engines separately. A synthetic pass establishes synthetic capability. Player-disjoint, opt-in human games would be a separate target with action prediction and calibration as the endpoints.
3. **Test consequences of *our* intervention.** Freeze the eligible observer before revealing a fresh block. From each held-out board choose a plausible intrusion or denial and a credible alternative. Run both branches against the actual opponent programs, including moves against a third seat. Before revealing replies, predict their distribution, which links get cut and eventual margin difference. Compare personalized, same-cost generic, shuffled and privileged predictions; include ordinary moves and cases where all policies agree. Require improved reply likelihood under intervention and positive paired realized margin for decisions changed by personalization, with positive impact when weighted by how often those positions occur. Select one native integration only after this passes.
4. **Pro first, Ultra after transfer.** Use the accepted native Pro incumbent at the time of the run; use v5.2-Pro if no successor has cleared confirmation. Screen one challenger against a frozen mixed league, then confirm it on fresh held-out opponents and seeds. Include style-stable, changing and unfamiliar opponents and a homogeneous three-Pentobi-L7 table. Rotate seats, use the same lineup/openings for paired arms, and charge feature extraction, observation, inference and search to the same wall-clock envelope. Log mean and p95 time, simulations per move and proportion of decisions changed. Compare incumbent, generic-policy search, wrong-history and personalized search. A separate run isolates the own-weight controller. Propose a 300-seed screen and an 800-fresh-seed confirmation for the primary league endpoint. Size external guardrail samples from pilot variance. Confirm positive lower 95% bound on paired margin against incumbent and generic, first-place share without a clear drop, and a predeclared L7 noninferiority margin (e.g. −0.5 points). Bootstrap whole seed/lineup blocks. Keep the ordinary Pentobi ladder as a distinct claim. Only after Pro passes, reprice the full system at Ultra and test at L8/L9. Frozen rules and a fresh holdout precede any tier naming.

The first milestone is one report with two answers: how much precise opponent knowledge could change our score on these positions, and how much of that information a model can recover soon enough to act. Those numbers decide whether to invest in better inference, better search, or the existing high-budget roadmap.
